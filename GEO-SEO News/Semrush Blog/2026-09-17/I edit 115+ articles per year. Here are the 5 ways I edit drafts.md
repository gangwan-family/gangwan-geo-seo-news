## I edit 115+ articles per year. Here are the 5 ways I edit drafts.

Author : Christine Skopec

8 min read

September 17, 2026

Contributor: Alex Lindley

Semrush publishes hundreds of blog articles every year. Each one needs editorial review, and most need revisions before they’re ready for publication. As a Senior Editor, I make that process happen.

What the editing for a given article actually entails is pretty varied. We cover a lot of topics, work with many writers, and balance competing priorities.

So, I simply can’t treat every piece the same way. But I also can’t invent a completely new approach whenever it’s time for me to edit content. (I did that initially, and it didn’t go well.)

Instead, I use one of five editing approaches. And I’m about to walk you through each of them, how I decide which one to use, and some actual examples of what those editing approaches look like. I’m also sharing my editing best practices for any piece — no matter the approach.

### My 5 editing approaches

Here’s how I define my five main editing approaches:

- Overhaul : An edit that’s almost exclusively comments to get the article directionally on track. There are usually some high-level edits in Google Docs’ Editing mode, such as rearranging the order of sections.

- Rework : A 30%-50% line-by-line edit in Editing mode with overarching comments for sections that need more substantial work. Alex Lindley, our Managing Editor, is the one who initially recommended a 30% edit for pieces that need a substantial number of revisions. I’m glad he did!

- Deep Dive : A line-by-line edit in Editing mode, with comments and revision requests throughout the entire article. This is easily what most content I edit falls under.

- Lighter Touch : An edit where most changes are straightforward. I apply changes either directly or in Editing mode, adding comments and revision requests as needed.

- No Notes : An edit where I apply all changes directly and add no commentary. These pieces are extremely rare.

I’m not normally one to coin terms — I hadn’t even thought to do so before I started writing this article. But using concrete names seems important for making sure you know what I’m talking about throughout the coming sections.

That said, you can call these editing approaches anything you want. I’m just not that precious about it.

### How I decide which editing approach to use

I choose an editing approach for an article by considering four dimensions in combination with one another:

- Article quality

- Article type

- Article importance

- Writer experience

I usually do this assessment in my head, but it translates pretty easily to a concrete framework that anyone who reviews content can use.

Here’s how it works: For the piece you’re working on, assign a score of 0 to 2 for each of the four dimensions based on the below framework. Add the scores for each column. Then add the column scores to get your total score.

Article quality

Significant issues across factual inaccuracy, tone of voice, structural coherence, clarity, search optimization, product mentions, and style guide adherence.

A few issues with factual inaccuracy, tone of voice, structural coherence, clarity, search optimization, product mentions, and style guide adherence.

Few if any issues with factual inaccuracy, tone of voice, structural coherence, clarity, search optimization, product mentions, and style guide adherence.

Article type

Fully rewritten version of an existing article

Refreshed version of an existing article

New URL

Critical level

Medium level

Low or unsure

Writer experience

This is a new writer. Or this writer has yet to submit a piece that’s close to expectations.

The writer has a decent understanding of what their content should look like. This can come from general familiarity with the brand’s content or from feedback they’ve received.

The writer has a clear understanding of how to create content that meets expectations. This can be from having substantial familiarity with the brand’s content or a longstanding relationship with the brand.

Column scores

Total score

Your total score determines which editing approach to take. That breakdown looks like this:

Score of 0 : Overhaul

Score of 1-2 : Rework

Score of 3-4 : Deep Dive

Score of 5-6 : Lighter Touch

Scores of 7-8 : No Notes

One thing to consider is that different brands can have different definitions of what makes a high-quality article. For example, not every company is as heavily focused as Semrush on content that drives search engine and AI visibility. So, make adjustments to that row to suit your needs.

And if you just launched your website? Take out the row for article type completely to accommodate having only new URLs. Then, adjust the scale to be from 0 to 6 at your own discretion.

### The benefit of using a framework to choose an editing approach

Using a decision framework to determine an editing approach improves efficiency and reduces mental fatigue.

I can usually determine what approach I’m taking in just a few minutes, which means I automatically know what to do once I start. I don’t have to ruminate over the details of what I’ll say or adjust because that all falls into place.

And as I continue working with the same writers, their articles naturally start to have higher scores. Which means the editing goes faster. And that I don’t need to provide such extensive feedback.

For example, I usually have to explain that we use the bottom line up front (BLUF) principle — stating the main takeaway at the beginning of every section — the first time I work with a writer. But by about their third article, they know what to do based on a comment that simply says, “Let’s BLUF this paragraph.”

My Rework approach is how I provide enough direction and feedback for a writer to make substantial revisions without the edit taking my entire day.

### Best practices regardless of the editing approach

#### Fact-check everything

Fact-checking is a nonnegotiable part of editing for everything that comes across my desk.

Because content I first review as an Overhaul or Rework can change substantially by the second draft, I typically only do line-by-line fact checking once I’ve received the revision.

And even if the article is great and I trust the writer, fact-checking still has to happen. It’s easy to misstate the findings from a study or say something related to SEO that’s almost true but not quite true. And those sorts of mistakes can break trust and hurt our reputation.

Common fact-checking tasks include:

- Verifying the spellings of individuals’ and companies’ names

- Ensuring specific numbers are supported and linking to the appropriate primary sources

- Cross-referencing findings covered in one or our written studies with the raw data

- Validating workflows and statements about tool features

- Finding support for assertions about SEO and AI visibility that I’m not certain about

#### Leave more feedback than you think you should

Leaving a substantial amount of feedback clarifies your expectations and helps writers understand why you’re making changes.

Any time you encounter a section that isn’t clear, contains unsubstantiated claims, or seems out of place, ask the writer about it.
