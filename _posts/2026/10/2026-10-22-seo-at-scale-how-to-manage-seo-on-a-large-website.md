## SEO at scale: How to manage SEO on a large website

Author : Carlos Silva

22 min read

October 22, 2026

Contributor: Faizan Ali

A site with 40,000 URLs and a two-person SEO team can't be managed page by page. You work with templates and rules that control thousands of pages at once.

In this guide, you’ll learn how to prioritize SEO work on a large website, make changes safely, and spot performance problems across different sections of your site.

### What SEO at scale means

SEO at scale is the process of managing a website’s organic search visibility when it has too many pages to optimize individually.

Most large sites fall into one of three types:

- Ecommerce : A retailer such as IKEA can have thousands of product pages, along with category pages that help shoppers browse those products

- Multi-location services : A company such as Mr. Rooter Plumbing has pages for different locations, with individual locations also covering the plumbing services they offer

- Software as a service (SaaS) : A software company such as Shopify can have documentation covering many features, tasks, and troubleshooting questions

The SEO basics still apply. Search engines need to find your important pages, understand their content, and determine when to show them in search results. But at scale, the challenge is applying those fundamentals consistently across thousands of pages.

I’ll focus on managing SEO issues and performance across a large website in this guide. If you want help producing content at scale, see our SEO content guide .

### How to manage SEO at scale

Managing SEO at scale comes down to five steps: organize your pages by type, prioritize the types that matter most, fix the problems they share, roll changes out safely, and monitor how each type performs.

The last four steps repeat. Large sites keep adding, changing, and removing pages, so what you see while monitoring sets your next priorities. Our enterprise SEO case studies show how other teams run this process.

Follow these steps to make SEO work manageable across thousands or even millions of pages:

#### 1. Organize SEO work by page type

On a large site, the template is your unit of work. If 4,000 category pages share one template, changing that template changes all 4,000 pages. Change one page and you've changed one.

A page type is a group of pages that serves the same purpose, such as product pages, category pages, location pages, or help articles. Pages of the same type usually share a template or follow the same rules, so grouping your URLs by page type is how you find the templates worth working on.

The exact page-type groups will vary by website.

Home Depot's sitemap shows several page types side by side: product categories such as "Electrical" and "Batteries," and project pages such as "Bathroom Ideas" and "Kitchen Ideas."

Mr. Rooter has location pages, service pages, and pages that combine a location with a specific service.

Shopify's Help Center is a single page type, help articles, organized into topics such as apps, products, payments, and point of sale.

To find these page types on your own site, start by looking at how your URLs are organized. You can use Semrush’s Site Audit , which can crawl up to 100,000 pages in a single audit and up to one million pages per month on the Business tier of the SEO Toolkit .

Create a Site Audit project for your domain, configure the audit , and then click “ Start audit .”

Go to the “ Crawled Pages ” report and click “ Site Structure .” This shows crawled URLs organized by subdomain and subfolder, along with the number of URLs in each section.

Look for folders that correspond to your main page types. For example:

- /products/ may contain product pages

- /collections/ may contain category pages

- /locations/ may contain location pages

- /blog/ may contain blog posts

Use these sections to create your main page-type groups.

If your URL structure doesn’t clearly separate page types, check how pages are classified in your content management system (CMS). Your CMS may already group pages as products, categories, articles, locations, or other page types.

#### 2. Prioritize page types by business impact

Rank each page type by how directly it contributes to revenue, leads, or another core business outcome.

What counts as high impact depends on your business type and how it makes money. For example:

- An ecommerce site like IKEA may treat product pages as high impact because they can directly lead to purchases, while ideas and inspiration articles may be lower impact if they mainly support research

- A multi-location service site like Mr. Rooter may treat service pages as high impact because they can generate calls or bookings, while blog posts may be lower impact if they mainly answer informational questions

- A SaaS site like Shopify may treat feature pages as high impact because they can influence signups or demo requests, while help center articles may be lower impact if they mainly support existing customers

In general, page types that sit closer to a purchase, booking, or qualified lead will often deserve more attention than pages that mainly support research or provide information.

So a page type that gets 40,000 visits but generates few conversions may deserve less attention than one that gets 3,000 visits and consistently drives sales or leads.

Use your analytics, ecommerce, customer relationship management (CRM), or other business data to see which page types generate revenue, leads, bookings, or other valuable conversions. Then assign each page type a priority such as high, medium, or low based on that contribution.

If your analytics setup can’t separate performance by page type, use Semrush’s Organic Rankings as a fallback. Enter the full subfolder URL for a page type (e.g. "example.com/products/") to see its estimated organic traffic and ranking keyword count. Repeat this for your main page types to compare their organic search performance.

Once you’ve prioritized the page types by business impact, use fixability to decide which of the high-impact groups to work on first.

A page type is easier to improve at scale when many affected URLs share a template, rule, or component that you can fix once.

A page type is harder to act on when the problems vary from page to page or fixing them would require a major redesign, migration, or large amount of manual work.

You can use Semrush’s Site Audit to check whether the same issue affects many URLs in a high-impact page type. Open the “ Issues ” report and click an issue to see all the URLs it affects.

Then use the “ Advanced filters ” option to enter the subfolder for one of your high-impact page types. For example, enter “/products/” for product pages. This lets you see whether the same issue appears across many URLs in that group rather than on only a few individual pages.

At the end of this step, every page type should have a priority from high to low, with the strongest combination of business impact and fixability at the top.

#### 3. Fix recurring SEO issues at scale

Fix recurring SEO issues in your highest-priority page types by identifying and correcting the shared cause.

Common issues you can address across groups of pages include:

##### Site taxonomy and URL structure

Site taxonomy is how you group pages into categories and subcategories, while URL structure is how those pages are represented in their web addresses.

For example, IKEA organizes its product categories within a clear hierarchy and uses consistent URL patterns for different page types.

On a large site, taxonomy and URL decisions can affect entire groups of pages. A poorly planned category or URL pattern can create overlapping pages, unnecessary levels in the hierarchy, or multiple URLs serving essentially the same purpose.

For each high-priority page type, review how its categories and subcategories are organized in your navigation or CMS. Then look for repeated URL patterns across that section. Check for problems such as:

- Categories or subcategories that substantially overlap

- Pages grouped under the wrong parent category

- Category levels that add little useful distinction

Suppose a site has both “/category/subcategory/” and “/subcategory/,” and both URLs represent the same category. Instead of maintaining both, the site could choose one structure, move the relevant items or pages under it, update internal links , and redirect the redundant URL to the one it keeps.

Depending on the exact issue you find, you may need to:

- Combine overlapping categories : Keep one category when two categories serve essentially the same purpose

- Move pages to a better-fitting category : Place pages under the category that best matches what they contain

- Remove unnecessary category levels : Simplify parts of the hierarchy that don’t add a useful distinction

- Use consistent URL patterns : Follow the same URL structure for pages of the same type

If the same URL-structure problem affects many pages of the same type, change the URL pattern in your CMS rather than editing each URL individually. Apply redirects using the same rule so affected URLs consistently point to the new structure.

##### Crawling and indexation

Crawling is how search engines discover and fetch your pages, while indexation is how eligible pages are added to a search engine’s index so they can appear in search results.

On a large site, you need to manage crawl budget so search engines spend more of their crawling resources on important pages. Thousands of unnecessary filters, parameters, or duplicate URLs can consume crawl budget and, if indexed, contribute to index bloat.

For example, an ecommerce category page may let shoppers filter products by size, color, brand, price, and availability. Different filter combinations can generate many URLs, even though many of those URLs don’t need to appear in search results.

Check your high-priority page types for:

- Access errors : 4xx or 5xx responses that prevent crawlers from reaching important pages

- Unreachable sitemap URLs : Pages listed in your sitemap that crawlers cannot access

- Crawl blocks : Important URLs blocked by robots.txt rules

- Indexing blocks : Important pages with a noindex directive that prevents them from appearing in search results

- Unnecessary URL variations : Filters or parameters that generate large numbers of crawlable URLs

- Pagination problems : Paginated pages without unique URLs or crawlable links to later pages

You can identify these issues in Semrush’s Site Audit . In the “Overview” tab, scroll to the “Thematic Reports” section and under “Crawlability,” click “ View details .”

Review “ HTTP Status Code ” for pages returning 4xx or 5xx errors. Then check “Sitemap vs. Crawled Pages” to see whether Site Audit could reach the URLs listed in your sitemap.

Next, go to the “ Issues ” tab and search “block” to find important URLs affected by crawl or indexing blocks.

Then go to the “ Crawled Pages ” tab and search for URL patterns created by filters or parameters, such as “?color=” in the URL. This can reveal large groups of URLs created by the same filter or parameter.

On larger or more complex sites, Semrush’s Enterprise Site Intelligence includes Bot Analytics, which uses server log data to show which URLs search-engine bots request and how often. This can confirm whether bots are reaching URLs from your high-priority page types or repeatedly crawling unnecessary URL patterns.

Depending on the issues you find, you may need to:

- Update crawl rules : Remove or change a robots.txt rule that blocks important URLs

- Fix access errors : Resolve page or server errors that stop crawlers from reaching important URLs

- Control filter URLs : Keep useful filter combinations indexable and prevent low-value combinations from being crawled

- Fix pagination : Give paginated pages unique URLs and connect them with crawlable links

- Fix noindex rules : Remove noindex directives from pages that should appear in search results

If the same crawl issue affects many URLs, fix the rule creating or exposing those URLs rather than handling them one by one. For example, change the link markup that generates crawlable filter URLs, or update the relevant robots.txt rule when you want to prevent crawlers from accessing a URL pattern.

##### Duplicate and near-duplicate content

Duplicate and near- duplicate content occurs when different URLs contain the same or very similar main content.

On large websites, this usually happens because the site creates multiple URLs from the same underlying content or page template.

Common causes include:

- Product variants : Different versions of the same product have separate URLs with largely similar content

- Filters and sorting options : Different URL combinations show many of the same products

- Similar categories : Separate category pages contain almost the same products and copy

For example, IKEA gives each variant of a product its own URL. This is the yellow version:

A different variant of the same product is available at another URL:

Much of the page content is the same across both URLs, while details such as the color, price, or material differ. Whether those variant URLs should remain indexable depends on search demand. If people search for a specific variant, such as “yellow wing chair,” keeping that page indexable may make sense. If a variant has little distinct search demand, you can use a canonical tag to indicate the preferred product URL.

When multiple near-duplicate URLs remain indexable, search engines still need to determine which version is most relevant for a query. They may show a different URL from the one you expect.

Duplicate URLs can also waste crawl resources and split ranking signals when links point to different versions instead of one preferred URL.

Start by finding duplicate and near-duplicate pages within the high-priority page types you identified earlier.

In Semrush’s Site Audit , go to the “ Issues ” tab and search “duplicate content” to see pages with at least 85% identical content.

Click the issue to see the affected URLs, then filter the list to the page type you’re reviewing, such as product or category pages.

Once you’ve identified the affected pages, group them by the pattern causing the duplication, such as product variants, filters, or similar categories, and decide how each pattern should be handled.

Depending on the exact issue you find, you may need to:

- Redirect or combine unnecessary duplicates : Keep one version when multiple pages serve essentially the same purpose

- Add or correct canonical tags : Tell search engines which URL you prefer as the main version when similar pages need to remain available

- Differentiate pages that need to rank separately : Update the shared template or CMS fields so pages can include distinct information such as product attributes, category details, or location-specific content

If duplicate URLs are being created across a page type, fix the shared source rather than correcting pages one by one. For example, update the canonical rule in the relevant template or change the URL-generation rule that creates the duplicate versions.

##### Multilingual SEO and hreflang

Hreflang helps search engines understand which version of a page is intended for users in a specific language or region.

For example, a website may have one version of a product page in French for users based in France and another in German for users based in Germany. Hreflang tells search engines which version is meant for each audience.

On large sites, one incorrect hreflang setup can affect many localized pages.

Check your high-priority page types for:

- Missing return links : One localized page points to another, but the other page does not point back

- Incorrect language or region codes : Hreflang uses a code that search engines don’t recognize, such as “en-UK” instead of the valid “en-GB”

- Invalid destination URLs : Hreflang points to a page that redirects, returns an error, or cannot be indexed

You can find these problems in Semrush’s Site Audit . In the “Overview” tab, scroll to the “Thematic Reports” section, and under “ International SEO ,” click “ View details .”

Review hreflang issues such as incorrect hreflang codes and other implementation errors. Click “ # issues ” to see the affected pages.

Then open "Hreflang conflicts within page source code" to see each affected page, its hreflang values, and the type of issue.

After identifying the affected pages, correct the hreflang tags by adding missing return links, fixing incorrect language or region codes, and replacing URLs that redirect or cannot be indexed. If the same error appears across many localized pages, update the shared rule that creates those hreflang tags rather than editing every page separately.

##### Internal linking

Internal links help users and search engines find related pages and move through your site.

On large sites, important pages can become orphaned, which means no internal links point to them. Or they may sit more than three clicks from the homepage, which indicates high crawl depth.

You can find internal link problems in Semrush’s Site Audit . In the “Overview” tab, scroll to the “Thematic Reports” section, and under “Internal Linking,” click “ View details .”

Review the crawl-depth chart to see how many pages sit several clicks from the homepage. Then check the errors, warnings, and notices for issues such as orphaned sitemap pages, broken internal links, and pages with only one internal link. Click “ # issues ” to see the affected URLs.

For orphaned pages that should remain on the site, add links from relevant pages that users and search engines can already reach. For deeply buried pages, create shorter paths from category, hub, or other prominent pages.

If many pages of the same type are affected, update the navigation or related-content section for that page type so you can add links across the group instead of page by page.

##### JavaScript rendering

JavaScript rendering is the process of running JavaScript code so the final content and links on a page can appear.

On a large site, a rendering problem in a shared template can affect every page that uses it.

For example, if a template relies on JavaScript to add internal links and those links don’t render correctly, search engines may have trouble finding the pages they lead to. Google can process JavaScript, but JavaScript-generated content and links still need to render in a format Google can access and crawl.

You can check whether JavaScript changes elements such as content, links, canonical tags, or meta robots directives with Semrush’s Site Audit . When JavaScript rendering is enabled, its “JS Impact” report shows what changes before and after JavaScript renders.

If the same rendering problem appears across many pages, fix the shared template or code causing it. Then rerun Site Audit to confirm the affected content or links now render correctly.

##### Structured data

Structured data is standardized code that tells search engines what specific information on a page means, such as a product’s price, availability, or rating.

It can make eligible pages appear as rich results that show some of those details directly in Google.

Structured data errors can spread across thousands of pages when the markup is generated from a shared template or data source.

For example, an ecommerce site may use its product template to add Product structured data to every product page. If the template pulls the wrong price or availability field, the same error can appear across the entire page type.

Look for recurring problems such as:

- Missing properties : The same required field is absent across many pages

- Invalid markup : The same structured data error appears repeatedly across one page type

- Incorrect values : Prices, availability, or other values don’t match the information shown on the page

You can find structured data problems in Semrush’s Site Audit . In the “Overview” tab, scroll to the “Thematic Reports” section and under “Markup,” click “ View details .”

Review the “Structured Data Items” section to see which markup types are valid or invalid. Open an invalid item to review the affected pages and the fields that need attention.

Look for the same error across URLs that share a page type. For example, if hundreds of "/product/" URLs are missing the same property, the problem likely comes from the product template or the data feeding that property.

Fix the shared source based on what you find.

- If a property is missing, add it to the template that generates the markup

- If a value is wrong, correct the CMS or product-catalog field supplying that value, or fix how that field maps to the structured data

Rerun Site Audit afterward to confirm the error has been resolved across the affected pages.

#### 4. Roll out changes safely

Roll out large SEO changes in stages so you can verify that they work as intended before applying them across the site.

A faulty large-scale change can cause rankings and organic traffic to drop across a large part of the site. Making several major changes at once can also make it harder to identify what caused the problem.

Use these safeguards when implementing changes across many pages:

- Set a pre-rollout baseline : Record organic traffic and indexed-page counts for each affected page type, and set up a Position Tracking campaign with tags for the keywords tied to each page type

- Prepare a rollback option : Keep the previous template version so you can restore it quickly if the rollout causes problems

- Group implementation by template : If a developer needs to make the change, create one ticket for the shared template instead of listing every affected URL

- Select an owner for each template : Decide who signs off on changes to each shared template, and keep a short checklist of the checks you run before every release

- Test and crawl the change in staging : Apply the update to a representative set of pages in a staging environment, then crawl the staging site with Site Audit to check for technical problems before launch

- Expand the rollout in batches : Once the test passes QA, apply the change to additional groups of pages rather than updating the entire site at once. This makes it easier to isolate a problem if one appears.

After every rollout, rerun Site Audit to check whether the change introduced new technical issues. Go to the “ Compare Crawls ” report to compare the latest crawl with the previous one and see which issues are new or fixed.

For larger sites, Semrush's Enterprise Site Intelligence monitors technical health continuously and flags new issues as they appear, so your team doesn't have to keep re-checking the site by hand.

##### Site migrations

A rebuild is the riskiest change a large site can make. Rankings often drop and can take months to recover, and when everything changes at once, you can't tell which change caused the drop.

Restructure one section at a time when you can. Migrate one page type or subfolder, check its crawlability, indexation, redirects, and organic traffic against your baseline, and fix any problems before you move on.

Whether you move one section or the whole site, these aren't optional:

- Crawl the old site first: Save every current URL and its crawl data so you can check the new site against it after launch

- Build a complete redirect map: Map every URL that changes to its new destination before anything moves

- Avoid redirect chains: Point old URLs straight to their final destination

After launch, crawl the new site and compare it with the old crawl to find missing pages and broken redirects. Our website migration checklist covers the full process.

#### 5. Monitor performance by segment

Once your changes are live, monitor the high-priority page types you worked on to see where SEO performance improves or declines.

Tracking those page types separately helps you spot changes that sitewide averages can hide. For example, overall organic traffic may stay flat even if product pages are losing traffic because growth in blog traffic offsets the decline.

For each high-priority page type, track metrics that show both search performance and business impact:

- Indexed pages : Track how many important URLs in each page type are included in Google’s index

- Organic traffic : See whether visits from search are increasing or decreasing

- Organic conversions : Track revenue, leads, bookings, or another valuable action generated by the page type

- Search visibility and rankings : Monitor whether the page type is gaining or losing visibility for its target keywords

In your analytics platform, group organic landing pages using the same URL patterns or CMS labels you established earlier. Compare each group’s organic visits and conversions with its pre-rollout baseline, accounting for seasonal changes.

Use Google Search Console to monitor which pages from each page type are indexed. Create separate XML sitemaps for major page types, such as product, category, or location pages, and submit them under “ Sitemaps .”

Then, go to “ Pages ” to see your page indexing report. Use the sitemap filter to select the sitemap for a page type.

Review how many URLs are indexed or not indexed, and check the reasons Google gives for pages it hasn’t indexed.

You can use Semrush’s Position Tracking to monitor where your site ranks for the keywords associated with each high-priority page type.

Add those keywords when you set up the campaign, then assign tags such as “Product pages” or “Category pages” to group them.

You can also add tags to keywords later from the “Rankings Overview” report.

Then open the “Tags” report to compare visibility, share of voice, estimated traffic, and average position across those groups.

Check which URLs rank for those keywords before attributing a visibility change to a page type.

If the keyword group tied to one page type loses visibility while the others remain stable, investigate that page type first. Check its crawl and indexation health to see whether a technical issue could be contributing to the decline.

##### Monitor AI search visibility

Monitor AI search visibility to make sure large-scale SEO changes aren’t reducing how often your brand is mentioned or your pages are cited in AI-generated answers.

Changes to crawlability, internal linking, or page structure can affect how easily AI crawlers discover and access your pages, which can affect their chances of being cited.

After any robots.txt, CDN, or firewall change, open Site Audit's "Crawled Pages" report and use the "Blocked AI search bots" filter to confirm AI crawlers can still reach your priority page types.

Track core signals:

- Mentions : Identify the prompts and topics where your brand appears

- Citations : Track how often your site is cited and which pages AI platforms use as sources

Pay particular attention to prompts related to the high-priority page types you changed and the pages cited for those prompts. If mentions or citations fall after a rollout, check whether the drop involves those page types.

### Automate repeatable SEO tasks

Automate repeatable SEO tasks that follow clear rules and recur across large parts of your site.

You can automate:

- Monitoring and alerts : Schedule technical checks and get notified when new issues appear

- Performance tracking : Automatically collect ranking, traffic, and AI visibility data instead of checking each page type manually

- Internal linking analysis : Automatically scan large groups of pages for orphaned pages, weak internal linking, or other recurring linking gaps

- Reporting : Generate recurring reports for different websites, markets, or business units

SEO tools can handle much of this recurring work once you configure what to monitor. For example, Site Audit can run scheduled crawls and Position Tracking can monitor search rankings and AI visibility over time.

Keep human review for changes that can affect large parts of the site, especially template changes and anything that creates or removes pages in bulk.

Programmatic SEO is one example of automation that still needs human review. It uses templates and data to create many related pages, so review those pages before publishing them at scale. Google may treat large-scale page creation as spam when its primary purpose is manipulating search rankings rather than helping users.

### Manage SEO at scale as an ongoing process

Large sites never stop changing, so this work doesn't finish. Keep working at the page-type level. Decide which types matter, fix what they share, roll changes out in batches, and let each type's results tell you what to fix next.

To start, open Site Audit 's "Crawled Pages" report and group your URLs by page type.

### Frequently asked questions

#### What counts as a large website for SEO?

There's no universal cutoff. Google's crawl budget guide is written for sites with 1 million+ unique pages that change weekly, or 10,000+ unique pages that change daily, and Google says those aren't exact thresholds.

A website may need SEO-at-scale processes earlier if reviewing and optimizing URLs one by one is no longer practical.

At the high end, it overlaps with enterprise SEO , which brings its own challenges around teams, approvals, and tooling.

#### What are the biggest challenges of SEO at scale?

The biggest challenges of SEO at scale are deciding what to prioritize, finding issues that affect many pages, rolling out changes safely, and tracking performance across large groups of URLs.

The larger the site, the more important it becomes to work on groups of similar pages rather than treating every URL as a separate SEO task.

#### Is programmatic SEO the same as SEO at scale?

No, programmatic SEO is not the same as SEO at scale.

Programmatic SEO uses automation, templates, and data to create large numbers of pages targeting related search queries. SEO at scale is broader and covers how you manage SEO across a large or complex website. Programmatic SEO can be one part of that process.

#### How do you avoid duplicate content on a large site?

You can avoid duplicate-content problems on a large site by limiting unnecessary URL variations and making it clear which version of similar pages search engines should treat as the main one.

Use redirects when duplicate URLs are no longer needed, canonical tags when similar URLs must remain accessible, and carefully manage filters, parameters, and regional versions that can create duplicate URLs.

#### How often should you audit a large website?

You should audit a large website at least once a week, and run additional audits after major changes such as migrations, template updates, or large content releases.

The right frequency depends on how often the site changes. A frequently updated site may need daily technical crawls, while a slower-moving site may only need weekly crawls.

#### How do you track SEO performance across thousands of pages?

Track SEO performance across thousands of pages by grouping similar pages and measuring each group separately instead of reviewing every URL individually.

For each group, monitor organic traffic, conversions, search visibility, and AI search visibility. This helps you see which parts of the site are improving, declining, or need attention next.
