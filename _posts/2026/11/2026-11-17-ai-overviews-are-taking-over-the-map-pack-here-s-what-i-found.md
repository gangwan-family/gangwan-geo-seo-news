## AI Overviews are taking over the map pack. Here’s what I found

Author : Chris Shirlow

15 min read

November 17, 2026

For most local businesses, appearing in the map pack has always been the main goal. Rank in the top three, and the calls come in. That’s why getting clients into the pack is such a big part of the work at my local SEO agency.

So when I was pulling numbers for one of our clients (an electrician), I searched for their main service myself. They were in the pack, right where we wanted them. But above it, Google’s AI Overview (AIO) recommended four other electricians. Our client wasn’t one of them.

That’s when it hit me: Are AIOs coming for local money keywords?

Up until then, most of the research I’d read looked at informational questions, like “do I need a permit to replace my roof in Austin?” Very little of it looked at the searches that bring in calls and booked jobs.

So I ran 480 of those searches by hand across 12 US markets over about two months. I wanted to know how often AIOs showed up, whether that proportion was changing, and whether they recommended the same businesses as the map pack.

By the end, I had my answer: AIOs weren’t coming for these searches. They were already here.

What I found changed how my agency approaches local SEO, and how I think about the map pack.

Key takeaways:

- AIOs showed up on 38% of high-intent local searches in my first round and 99% in my last, about two months later

- In my last round of testing, the AIO completely replaced the map pack, showing local businesses as cards inside the AIO instead

- When the AIO and the map pack shared a page, about one in five AIOs didn’t name a single pack business

- Conversational searches like “need an emergency plumber [city]” got AIOs before bare ones like “emergency plumber [city]”

- More than a third of the businesses the AIO recommended didn’t have a website ranking on page one

### How I ran the study

To see what a real customer sees, I ran every search by hand. I searched 12 US cities across four sizes and four regions:

Here’s how I conducted my study:

- Services : 10 types of local businesses across home services, legal, healthcare, and restaurants

- Two ways to search : Each service was searched as a bare keyword (“emergency plumber [city]”) and as a conversational version (“need an emergency plumber [city]”)

- How I captured each page : Signed out, in an incognito window, with the full results page saved and the AIO expanded

- Timing : Every market was searched twice, between September and November 2026

- What I logged : Whether an AIO appeared, where it sat compared with the map pack , which businesses it named and linked, and how many of those businesses were also in the pack

That’s 480 searches in total: 12 markets, 10 services, two ways to search, and two rounds each.

### AI Overviews spread to nearly every high-intent local search during my study

AIOs went from showing up on about a third of high-intent local searches to nearly all of them while I ran this study. In my first round, 38% of searches showed an AIO. By my last round, 99% (79 of 80) showed them.

Because I searched every market twice, I could compare the same searches over time. Both times, AIO appearances increased.

In the first eight markets, the rate went from 38% to 66% in about two weeks. In the last four, it went from 60% to 99% in about four weeks. Across all 480 searches, Google showed an AIO on 292 (61%).

Worcester, Massachusetts, was the most dramatic increase. On the first pass, only one of its 20 searches had an AIO. On the second, the same 20 searches had 12. Philadelphia, Pennsylvania went from 4 to 19.

And once an AIO showed up, it usually stuck around. Of the 108 AIOs in my first pass, 106 were still there the second time.

So if you checked your keywords a few months ago and didn’t see AIOs, check again.

### AI Overviews can replace the map pack

In my last round of searches, the AIO completely replaced the map pack. On 79 of 80 searches, there was no separate map pack at all. Instead, the local businesses showed up as cards inside the AIO.

That was new. When I first searched these four markets, every AIO sat above a regular map pack. About four weeks later, the pack was gone:

The cards look a lot like map pack listings. Each one shows the business name, rating, and review count, plus buttons to call, get directions, or visit the website.

But the cards weren’t just the old pack in a new spot. The AIO named about five businesses per search, compared with the pack’s three. Most of the businesses from the first-pass map packs made it in: 154 of 236 (65%). But about a third didn’t, and on 9 of the 79 searches, none of them did. Nashville kept the fewest, at 42%.

The new layout also made room for smaller businesses. About one in five cards (81 of 379) belonged to a business with fewer than 50 reviews. On “need an emergency plumber” in Cottonwood, Arizona, the first card was a plumber with one review.

I captured this round on a single day, so it could be a test or a rollout that hasn’t reached everyone yet. But it fits the pattern I saw throughout the study: as time went on, AIOs took up more and more of the page.

If this layout sticks, there might be no separate pack left to rank in. Getting into the AIO would become the main way to show up at the top of local search results.

### AI Overviews often recommend different businesses than the map pack

When the AIO and the map pack shared a page, they often recommended different businesses. That was the layout on every AIO until my last round.

On the 145 searches where the AIO appeared above the map pack, it left out at least one pack business 114 times (79%). Usually, that meant the AIO presented a mix: it named one or two businesses from the pack, then added others that weren’t in it.

So about one in five times, the AIO didn’t name a single business from the pack. And only about one in five times did it include all three.

The AIO also named more businesses than the pack did: about four per answer, compared with the pack’s three. That gives businesses outside the pack a real chance to show up.

The businesses the AIO linked in its opening sentence were the least likely to be in the pack. I started tracking those links partway through the study. In 26 of the 70 searches where I could compare them (37%), the opening sentence didn’t link a single business from the pack.

On a “need a dentist” search in Phoenix, for example, the opening sentence linked Downtown Smiles, which wasn’t in the pack. Two of the pack’s three dentists showed up further down the list.

That opening sentence is the first thing a searcher reads, and on searches like this one, it pointed them to a business outside the pack before any of the pack’s picks.

So, ranking in the map pack doesn’t guarantee a spot in the AIO. My electrician client is one example. It works the other way too: plenty of businesses that weren’t in the pack still got recommended in the AIO.

#### Some AI Overviews just repeat the map pack

A small share of AIOs didn’t give their own answer at all. They sat below the map pack and named the same three businesses. I call these “pack echoes.”

They weren’t common. Only 38 AIOs were echoes, and all of them came from just three markets: Phoenix, Philadelphia, and Thomasville. Once the AIO replaced the map pack, pack echoes disappeared, since there was no pack left to repeat.

### Conversational local searches got AI Overviews first

Conversational searches got AIOs first, and bare searches caught up later. On my first pass, conversational searches got an AIO 66% of the time, compared with 24% for bare ones. By the second pass, bare searches had reached 66%, the same rate conversational searches had on the first pass.

The difference between the two came down to one small change: adding “need a” to the front of a regular search, so “emergency plumber [city]” became “need an emergency plumber [city].”

The gap showed up almost everywhere. Conversational searches got more AIOs than bare ones in 19 of the 24 market rounds.

Toledo, Ohio, shows the whole pattern. On the first pass, all 10 conversational searches had an AIO, compared with two of the 10 bare searches. On the second pass, all 20 did. Estate planning followed the same path across all 12 markets: on the first pass, 11 of 12 conversational searches had an AIO, compared with two of 12 bare ones.

The way people searched also changed what kind of AIO they were shown. When the map pack was still on the page, 83% of conversational AIOs were full answers with their own recommendations, compared with 36% of bare ones.

So if your bare keywords don’t show an AIO yet, check the conversational versions. In my study, they got AIOs first.

### What local businesses should do to show up in AI Overviews

To show up in AIOs, local businesses need to treat them as the main place local results live, not an extra feature next to the map pack. Ranking in the pack never guaranteed a spot: when the two shared a page, about one in five AIOs didn’t name a single pack business. By my last round, the AIO had taken the map pack’s place at the top of the page.

Here’s what the data points to.

#### Keep your Google Business Profile accurate

Keeping your GBP accurate matters because the business cards inside AIOs pull from it. They showed the same names, ratings, and addresses as GBP listings, and some AIOs also cited GBPs as sources.

So when listings get messy, the AIO can, too. On one Spokane plumber search, it showed two plumbers with different names but an identical photo, address, and rating. Whether that’s a mix-up or two connected businesses, it’s a sign that overlapping listings make it harder for Google to tell businesses apart.

Accurate listings matter even more now that the cards are the local results. Goettl’s High Desert Mechanical has two offices near Cottonwood, Arizona, and each has its own Google listing. When I searched “emergency plumber cottonwood az,” the AIO showed the Cottonwood office. When I searched “need an emergency plumber cottonwood az,” it showed the Camp Verde office instead. Same business and same town, but a different listing just from rewording the search.

Choose accurate primary and secondary categories, and keep your business name, address, and phone number the same everywhere they appear online. If you have more than one location, make sure each listing is complete, since you can’t control which one shows up.

#### Build a page for every city you serve

Building a page for every city you serve can help your visibility, because many of the businesses the AIO recommended had one (even ones based somewhere else):

- Re-Bath and Bath Fitter : National brands whose Toledo, Ohio, pages the AIO linked to directly

- Nicole Pavlik Law Firm : A Phoenix firm that showed up in a Cottonwood, Arizona, estate planning AIO

- Martin, Birdsong & Andreasen : An Osage Beach firm that showed up in Marshall, Missouri, personal injury and estate planning AIOs, about 100 miles away

If you serve more than one city, build a location page or service area page for each one. Each page should say what you do there, which neighborhoods you cover, and how to reach you.

#### Get on third-party “best of” lists

More than a third of the businesses the AIO recommended didn’t have a page-one website. So when I checked the sources it cited, many were third-party sites: review sites, directories, and “best of” lists.

Sometimes the source was a list. On my first pass, for “need a roofing contractor” in Cedar Park, Texas, every business the AIO named appears to come from a single “5 best roofing companies in Cedar Park” blog post. That one page decided the whole answer. And notice that none of the four roofers it picked are actually based in Cedar Park.

On my second pass, the same search named five different roofers, and two of them were based in Cedar Park. That “5 best” list was still one of the sources the AIO cited. A single list can get you into the answer, but it can’t keep you there.

Look for the “best [service] in [city]” lists, local roundups, and industry directories that already rank in your market, and work on getting included. After business websites, AIOs cited Yelp most often, followed by industry directories like Justia for lawyers and Zocdoc for healthcare, plus Facebook and Reddit.

To find the gaps, search your main services the way customers do, then open the sources the AIO cites. If a list or directory names your competitors but not you, that’s your next outreach target.

#### Write for the way people really search

Write your pages for the way customers talk. In my study, conversational searches got more AIOs than bare ones, so it pays to answer the questions customers actually ask, in the words they use.

I’ve been watching this play out for a while. When AIOs first rolled out, clients kept asking me if we could optimize their sites for AI. My honest answer was “yes, but...” In my testing, people searching for my clients’ services still weren’t being shown AIOs.

So I started testing different ways of searching to see what would trigger one. After a lot of trial and error, I found that conversational searches like “who is the best [service] company in [city]?” pulled up an AIO most of the time. I started adding conversational questions as an FAQ on client pages, and I still do.

Spell out your services, the areas you serve, what jobs typically cost, and your credentials. Then add the questions people actually ask, and answer them directly on the page.

For example:

- “How much does it cost to replace a water heater in [city]?”

- “Do you offer same-day AC repair in [neighborhood]?”

- “Do I need a lawyer after a minor car accident in [state]?”

Then, back up those details with LocalBusiness schema markup (or a specific type like Plumber or Dentist), including your name, address, hours, and service area. Schema gives Google the same information in a format it can read easily. Just make sure it matches what’s on the page.

The same thing kept happening in my last round of testing: both personal injury searches in Cedar Park, Texas, opened with The Clein Law Firm, which had four reviews. Firms with dozens to more than 140 reviews came after it.

So a big review count won’t keep you in the AIO on its own. Keep earning reviews, and pair them with the city pages and third-party mentions above.

#### Search your top services the way customers do

Search your main services regularly, because AIOs changed a lot over the two months of my study. Try both the bare keyword (e.g., “[service] [city]”) as well as the way a customer might ask conversationally (e.g., “who’s the best [service] in [city]?”). Note whether you show up in the AIO, the map pack (if there is one), both, or neither.

### FAQs

#### How do AI Overviews affect local SEO?

AI Overviews affect local SEO by recommending businesses at the top of local searches, sometimes different ones than the map pack. In my study, AIOs went from appearing in 38% of searches in my first round to 99% in my last, and in the latest round, they replaced the map pack entirely. So ranking in the map pack alone no longer guarantees searchers see you first.

#### Do Google AI Overviews use Google Business Profile data for local searches?

Yes, Google AI Overviews appear to use Google Business Profile data for local searches. In my study, the business cards inside AIOs showed the same names, ratings, and addresses as GBP listings, and some cited GBPs as sources. Keeping your profile accurate and consistent helps the AIO get your details right.

#### How do I get my local business into AI Overviews?

To get your local business into AI Overviews, keep your GBP accurate, build a page for every city you serve, get listed on the “best of” lists and directories that AIOs cite, and answer customers’ questions in their own words. In my study, conversational searches got AIOs first, so content written the way people talk can help you show up early.

### Winning the map pack isn’t enough anymore

Winning the map pack isn’t enough anymore. In about two months, AIOs went from showing up on some high-intent local searches to nearly all of them. They don’t always recommend the same businesses as the pack, and in my latest round, they replaced it entirely.

The upside is that what the AIO shows isn’t locked in. Clean listings, city pages, “best of” mentions, and content that answers real questions can get you in. And because AIOs are still spreading, starting now puts you ahead of competitors who are only watching the map pack.

The work that gets you into the pack still helps, so keep at it. Just make sure you’re earning a spot in the AIO, too. Semrush’s Position Tracking can show you where you stand in both.
