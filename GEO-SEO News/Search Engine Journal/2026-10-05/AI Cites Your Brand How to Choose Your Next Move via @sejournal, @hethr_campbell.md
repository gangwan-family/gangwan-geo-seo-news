An AI answer mentions your brand. That looks like progress, but it does not tell you what to do next. A competitor may appear more often, the answer may describe an old version of your product, or important pages may never reach the bots collecting information.

In this webinar, Constance Tan, Product Marketer at Ahrefs, explained how to separate those problems and choose the right response. Her starting point: look for recurring patterns across the customer journey, not a reassuring mention in one prompt.

### Track Questions That Reflect How Customers Choose

Generating hundreds of prompts does not guarantee useful coverage. Tan organized monitoring around three kinds of questions:

- Problems customers need to solve. These capture discovery before someone knows which brand to consider.

- Positioning and comparisons. These reveal which brands AI recommends for particular audiences, use cases, and categories.

- Facts about your business. These test whether answers accurately describe pricing, capabilities, availability, and product use.

Search queries, support conversations, sales questions, and relevant community discussions can supply the language for those prompts. Tan cautioned that the most useful forums differ by market; Reddit and Quora are not the answer everywhere.

Constance Tan described the goal this way:

“The idea is that you want a representative view from every angle of the customer journey.”

Monitor that set over time. Repeated sources, positioning claims, and factual errors give you something concrete to investigate.

### Follow Competitor Mentions Back to Their Sources

If competitors appear more often, share of voice identifies the gap. Reading the answers helps explain it. Does AI repeatedly favor another brand for small businesses or ecommerce? Which pages supply those distinctions, and does your content explain the same use cases?

Tan recommended examining both the sources and their formats. Reviews, discussions, and videos can matter alongside articles. For outreach, prioritize pages cited repeatedly and authors you can realistically reach. Domain strength and organic traffic add context, but an influential competitor-owned page may offer little opportunity for a correction.

The recording’s competitive-source walkthrough shows how Tan starts with a side-by-side visibility comparison and narrows it down to the specific sources worth digging into.

### Fix Inaccurate Information Where You Have Control

Before commissioning another article, inspect your own pricing pages, product explanations, profiles, and older posts. Conflicting information can leave AI answers describing features or plans that have changed.

Third-party corrections require more patience . Tan shared an Ahrefs outreach example: the team contacted 26 authors about inaccurate information, 10 replied, and four updated their content. One issue concerned older descriptions of which plans included API access.

Those are outreach outcomes, not proof of a corresponding lift in citations or revenue. They illustrate why selecting reachable, frequently cited sources matters.

Updating someone else’s page is not always practical. Constance Tan explained the alternative:

“Sometimes outreach is not always the answer. Sometimes it’s better to create the new sources of information, new pages that answer or cover the topic in a better way, a more comprehensive way, or with more up-to-date information.”

In the Q&A, Tan expanded on that choice: a warm relationship can make a correction worthwhile, while an important topic with weak coverage may justify an original guide or collaboration. If the same error appears across several sources, one new article may not be enough.

### Earn Useful Mentions, and Check Bot Access

Tan’s advice for community participation was not to insert a product pitch into every thread. Answer technical questions, correct factual mistakes, or offer useful guidance. Recurring complaints can also reveal product or onboarding problems worth taking back to the teams that can fix them.

Missing citations can have a different cause entirely: bots may be unable to retrieve the content. Tan recommended checking firewall restrictions, broken URLs, timeouts, and pages that rely on JavaScript to display important information.

Investigate those failures before treating every visibility gap as a content problem. A useful page cannot serve as a retrieved source if the bot cannot access its information.

### Turn the Findings Into Your Next Round of Work

Visibility reporting also needs business context. Asked about revenue, Tan discussed Ahrefs’ self-reported discovery data, including customers who mentioned ChatGPT, rather than claiming a revenue-per-citation formula. Her recommendation was to consider impressions and share of voice alongside conversions, sales, and customer attribution information.

Watch the full session for the source comparisons, outreach examples, and bot-access checks. To put the approach into practice, start with one customer segment and use what you find to choose a specific action:

- Build a balanced prompt set. Cover customer problems, comparisons, and factual questions using language from search and customer conversations.

- Investigate repeated claims. Identify the sources behind recurring recommendations or errors instead of reacting to every isolated answer.

- Correct owned information first. Update outdated product and pricing explanations, then prioritize third-party corrections you can realistically secure.

- Match the fix to the problem. Use outreach for reachable sources, useful new content for coverage gaps, and technical checks for retrieval failures.

- Review visibility with business results. Track patterns over time alongside conversions and customer feedback, without treating a citation as a sale.
